FRC for Parents

What the game is, how scoring works, and how to follow along

Your student is on FRC Team 5940 “BREAD”, a FIRST Robotics Competition team. Every January, thousands of high school teams worldwide get the same brand-new game and the same deadline: design, build and program a 120-pound robot to play it. Then they compete.

This page explains the basics in plain English — no robotics background needed. You can read the whole thing in about five minutes, or jump to How to follow along if you just want to watch.

The big idea

Two alliances — three robots on red, three on blue — play a 2 minute 40 second match on a field the size of a basketball court. Robots are driven by students from behind a wall; for the first 20 seconds they run entirely on pre-written code with no driver input at all.

Teams are shuffled into different alliances each match, so the team you're playing with in one match may be the team you play against in the next. That's intentional: the program calls it “Coopertition,” and it's why you'll see teams lending each other parts in the pits minutes before competing.

The 2026 game: REBUILT

Each season has its own game. The 2026 game is REBUILT, presented by Haas, and it works like this:

FUEL

Yellow foam balls, about 6 inches across. There are 504 of them on the field. This is the only game piece, and robots shoot them.

The HUB

Each alliance's goal. Robots shoot FUEL through the opening at the top. Every ball that goes in is worth 1 point — so scores run high, often past 100.

The TOWER

A ladder-shaped structure. In the closing seconds, robots climb it. The higher they get, the more points — up to 30 for one robot.

The twist: your HUB turns off

This is the part that confuses most first-time spectators, so it's worth understanding — it explains why robots sometimes appear to stop scoring on purpose.

During the middle of the match, the two alliances take turns. Only one alliance's HUB is active at a time. Score into an inactive HUB and you get nothing. So while your HUB is off, the smart play is to gather FUEL and defend — block the other alliance, get in their way, and stockpile for when your HUB switches back on.

Which alliance sits out first is decided by the autonomous period: whoever scores more FUEL in those opening 20 seconds has their HUB switched off first. A strong auto is still worth it, but it hands the opponent the first turn.

How a match unfolds

  1. 0:20 Autonomous No drivers. The robot runs pre-written code on its own. Both HUBs are active. A robot can climb to Level 1 of the TOWER here for 15 points.
  2. 0:10 Transition Drivers take the controls. Both HUBs still active — a brief free-for-all.
  3. 4 × 0:25 Alliance Shifts The alternating on/off period described above. One alliance scores while the other gathers and defends, then they swap. Four times.
  4. 0:30 End Game Both HUBs active again, and robots head for the TOWER to climb. This is usually the loudest part of the match.

Total: 2 minutes 40 seconds. Matches run back-to-back all day, so an event is a long one — bring a water bottle and ear protection if you're sensitive to noise.

Winning isn't the only thing that counts

During the qualification rounds, teams are ranked by Ranking Points (RP), not by score. You'll see RP on the dashboard's Team Status card.

  • 3 RP — win the match
  • 1 RP — tie
  • 0 RP — lose
  • +1 RP — ENERGIZED: your alliance scores at least 100 FUEL
  • +1 RP — SUPERCHARGED: your alliance scores at least 360 FUEL
  • +1 RP — TRAVERSAL: your alliance earns at least 50 TOWER points

This is why an alliance that's clearly losing will still fight for one more climb or one more armful of FUEL in the final seconds — a bonus RP is worth real ground in the standings even in a loss.

How a competition weekend works

  1. Qualification matches. Every team plays roughly 10 matches with randomly assigned partners. These set the rankings.
  2. Alliance selection. The top-ranked teams become alliance captains and take turns picking partners, live, in front of everyone. Getting picked is a big deal — it means other teams rate your robot.
  3. Playoffs. The alliances play a bracket until one wins the event.
  4. Awards. Judged awards recognize engineering design, outreach, and team culture. Several rank above winning on the field — the Impact Award is the program's highest honor and has nothing to do with match record.

Right now: Chezy Champs

Chezy Champs runs September 18–20, 2026 at Bellarmine College Preparatory in San Jose, CA. It's an offseason event — it doesn't affect official standings or championship qualification.

That doesn't mean it's casual. Chezy Champs is hosted by Team 254 and is one of the most competitive events of the year, because it invites a strong field and lets teams run robots they've spent all summer improving. It's a great first event to watch: the robots are more polished than they were in the spring, and the atmosphere is looser. The playoff bracket here runs 16 alliances.

How to follow along

The FRC dashboard on this site tracks BREAD live. A few things worth setting up before match day:

  • Match reminders. Click Enable Notifications on the dashboard and your browser will alert you before each BREAD match, with a link straight to the live stream. You can choose how much warning you get (2 to 15 minutes) and optionally get an alert when each match is scored. These alerts need the dashboard tab left open.
  • Calendar entries. Add to Calendar downloads the whole match schedule with alarms built in — this works even with the tab closed and is the more reliable option if you'll be away from your computer. There's also a one-click Google Calendar import.
  • Live stream. When the event is running, the stream appears directly on the dashboard.
  • Win predictions. The percentage next to each match is a statistical estimate from The Blue Alliance based on past performance. It is a forecast, not a verdict — upsets are routine and are most of the fun.

Words you'll hear

Alliance
The three teams playing together in a match.
Auto / Autonomous
The opening 20 seconds, robot-controlled, no drivers.
Teleop
The rest of the match, driven by students.
Quals
Qualification matches — the round-robin that sets rankings.
RP
Ranking Points. What the standings are actually sorted by.
OPR
A statistic estimating how many points a team contributes per match.
The pits
The work area where teams repair robots between matches. Open to spectators — safety glasses required, and worth a walk through.
Scouting
Students recording data on every robot to inform alliance picks.
Bag / Load-in
Moving the robot and toolboxes into the venue before the event.

Questions are welcome — ask your student. Being asked to explain the game is one of the better things that can happen to a rookie on a robotics team.
